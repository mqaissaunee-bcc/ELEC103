11.1What changes when the current alternates

Everything so far has been direct current: the source pushes in one direction and the current holds steady. reverses direction periodically, and its magnitude changes continuously as it does.

That single difference is why this section sits in the middle of your semester. The oscilloscope, the function generator, and the frequency counter — the analog instruments your lab has been promising — exist because a changing quantity cannot be read off a meter as a single number.

Why the wall socket alternates

Alternating voltage can be raised and lowered by a transformer, which is a passive device with no moving parts. That makes it cheap to send power long distances at high voltage and low current, then step it down near the point of use. Direct current has no equally simple equivalent, which is the whole reason the grid runs on AC.

The sine wave

The fundamental AC waveform is the . It is what a rotating generator naturally produces, and it has a property no other waveform shares: pass a sine wave through any combination of resistors, capacitors, and inductors and what comes out is still a sine wave, possibly shifted or scaled but never reshaped. Every other waveform can be built from sine waves added together.

11.2Period and frequency

One complete repetition of a waveform is a . The time one cycle takes is the , symbol T, measured in seconds. The number of cycles per second is the , symbol f, measured in hertz.

They are reciprocals, and that is the entire relationship:

f = 1 / T     T = 1 / f

Worked example 1

A waveform has a period of 1 ms. What is its frequency?

f = 1 / T f = 1 ÷ 0.001 s f = 1 000 Hz = 1 kHz

Worked example 2

A signal is at 50 kHz. What is its period?

T = 1 / f T = 1 ÷ 50 000 Hz T = 0.000 02 s = 20 µs

The prefix pairs worth memorizing

Because they are reciprocals, the prefixes pair up predictably. A period in milliseconds gives a frequency in kilohertz; a period in microseconds gives megahertz. 1 ms ↔ 1 kHz. 1 µs ↔ 1 MHz. Recognizing those two pairs turns most of these problems into mental arithmetic.

Guided problem 1 · period to frequency

A waveform has a period of 40 µs. What is its frequency in hertz?

  • Period and frequency are reciprocals, so one is simply 1 divided by the other.
  • Convert the period to seconds before taking the reciprocal, or the answer will be out by a large factor.
  1. Convert first: 40 µs = 40 × 10⁻⁶ s = 0.000 040 s.
  2. Take the reciprocal: f = 1 ÷ 0.000 040.
  3. That gives 25 000.
  4. The frequency is 25 000 Hz, or 25 kHz. Note the pairing: microseconds and megahertz, milliseconds and kilohertz — 40 µs sits between, at 25 kHz.

Guided problem 2 · frequency to period

A function generator is set to 2.5 kHz. What is the period of its output in microseconds?

  • Start from T = 1/f and work in base units, then convert the answer at the end.
  • A period in the hundreds of microseconds corresponds to a frequency in the low kilohertz.
  1. Convert the frequency: 2.5 kHz = 2 500 Hz.
  2. T = 1 ÷ 2 500 = 0.0004 s.
  3. Convert to microseconds: 0.0004 s × 10⁶ = 400.
  4. The period is 400 µs. As a check, 400 µs is 0.4 ms, and 1 ms pairs with 1 kHz, so 0.4 ms should pair with 2.5 kHz — it does.

11.3Four ways to state the same amplitude

A sine wave has no single value — it is different at every instant. So we quote it four different ways, and which one you mean matters enormously.

  • (Vp) — from zero to the highest point.
  • (Vpp) — from the lowest point to the highest. Twice the peak.
  • (Vrms) — the DC voltage that would produce the same heating. 0.707 times the peak.
  • — over a half cycle, 0.637 times the peak. Over a full cycle it is zero, which is why the half-cycle figure is the one quoted.

Why RMS is the one that counts

RMS stands for root mean square, which describes how it is calculated, but the meaning is what matters: it is the equivalent DC value for heating. A 10 V RMS sine wave delivers exactly as much power to a resistor as 10 V of DC would. Every other value overstates or understates the work the waveform actually does.

This is why AC quantities are quoted in RMS by default. The 120 V at a wall socket is RMS; its peak is about 170 V and its peak-to-peak swing is about 340 V. When a specification says volts without qualifying it, assume RMS.

Your meter and your scope disagree, and both are right

A DMM on its AC range reports RMS. An oscilloscope shows you the waveform, from which you naturally read peak-to-peak. Measure the same signal with both and the numbers differ by a factor of about 2.83 — that is 2 × 0.707 upside down, not an instrument fault. Students lose a lot of lab time to this. Note which quantity each instrument gave you, every time.

Guided problem 3 · peak to RMS

A sine wave has a peak value of 6.0 V. What is its RMS value?

  • For a sine wave, the RMS value is a fixed fraction of the peak.
  • That fraction is 0.707. Multiply, do not divide.
  1. RMS = 0.707 × peak.
  2. Substitute: 0.707 × 6.0.
  3. That gives 4.242.
  4. The RMS value is 4.24 V. This is the DC voltage that would heat a resistor by the same amount, and it is what a DMM on its AC range would display.

Guided problem 4 · RMS back to peak-to-peak

A DMM on its AC range reads 12.0 V RMS on a sine wave. If you put the same signal on an oscilloscope, what peak-to-peak voltage would you measure?

  • You are going backwards here: from RMS to peak, and then from peak to peak-to-peak. Two steps, not one.
  • Going from RMS to peak means dividing by 0.707, which makes the number bigger.
  1. First find the peak: peak = RMS ÷ 0.707 = 12.0 ÷ 0.707 = 16.97 V.
  2. Peak-to-peak is twice the peak.
  3. 2 × 16.97 = 33.94.
  4. The scope would show 33.9 V peak-to-peak. The two instruments differ by a factor of about 2.83 on the same signal, and neither is wrong.

11.4The function generator

The is your source of AC signals. It produces a waveform whose shape, frequency, and amplitude you set independently, and on most models it can add a as well.

The controls you will actually use

  • Waveform. Sine, square, or triangle. Sine unless the lab says otherwise.
  • Frequency. Usually a coarse range switch plus a fine dial, or a keypad on a digital model.
  • Amplitude. Sets the output level. Read the units on the display — some generators show peak-to-peak, some show RMS.
  • DC offset. Shifts the whole waveform up or down. Leave it at zero unless you need it.
  • Output impedance. Typically 50 Ω. It matters more in later courses, but it is why the displayed amplitude can differ from what the scope shows into some loads.

Verify the output on the scope

The generator's display tells you what it was asked for, not necessarily what is arriving at your circuit. Get in the habit of confirming amplitude and frequency on the oscilloscope before you record anything. This is the same lesson as measuring the DC supply rather than trusting its dial, from section 1.6.

Arbitrary function generators

A newer generation of instruments can output any waveform you define, point by point, rather than only the three built-in shapes. You may see one in the lab. For this course, the standard three are all you need.

Reading a frequency counter

A frequency counter counts cycles over a known time window and displays the result directly. It is far more accurate than reading a period off a scope screen and dividing. If the lab has one, use it for the frequency and use the scope for the amplitude — each instrument to its strength.

Guided problem 5 · what the generator means by its setting

A function generator is set to produce a 10.0 V peak-to-peak sine wave. You connect a DMM on its AC range across the output. What voltage will the meter display?

  • The generator is quoting peak-to-peak. The meter reports RMS. Convert between them.
  • Get from peak-to-peak to peak first, then apply the 0.707 factor.
  1. Peak = peak-to-peak ÷ 2 = 10.0 ÷ 2 = 5.0 V.
  2. RMS = 0.707 × peak = 0.707 × 5.0.
  3. That gives 3.535.
  4. The meter will read 3.54 V. This mismatch is why you should always check which quantity your generator’s display is quoting before recording anything.

11.5Waveforms that are not sine waves

Your generator makes three shapes, and digital circuits are full of a fourth. Each has vocabulary attached that you will meet in lab instructions.

Waveform shapes

Pulse vocabulary

A pulse waveform spends part of each period high and the rest low. The fraction of the period spent high is the , expressed as a percentage:

duty cycle = (pulse width ÷ period) × 100%

A square wave is the special case of a 50% duty cycle. Real pulses do not switch instantly, so two more terms describe the edges: is how long the leading edge takes to go from 10% to 90% of its full height, and fall time is the same measurement on the trailing edge.

Superimposed DC

An AC waveform can ride on top of a DC level. Add 5 V of DC offset to a 2 V peak sine wave and the result swings between 3 V and 7 V, never going negative at all. This is extremely common in real circuits — the signal inside an amplifier is usually an AC waveform sitting on a DC operating point.

This is what AC coupling is for

Your oscilloscope has a . On DC coupling it shows the whole signal, offset and all. On AC coupling it blocks the DC and shows only the varying part, which lets you look at a small ripple riding on a large DC level without the trace flying off the screen. Section 11.6 lets you try it.

Guided problem 6 · duty cycle from a waveform

The pulse waveform below is displayed with the timebase at 1 ms per division. What is its duty cycle as a percentage?

  • Duty cycle is the fraction of each period that the waveform spends high, expressed as a percentage.
  • Count divisions rather than trying to judge time by eye. How many divisions is one full cycle, and how many of those is the pulse high?
  1. One complete cycle occupies 4 divisions, so the period is 4 × 1 ms = 4 ms.
  2. The waveform is high for 1 division, so the pulse width is 1 ms.
  3. Duty cycle = width ÷ period × 100 = 1 ÷ 4 × 100.
  4. The duty cycle is 25%. A square wave would be the special case of 50%.

11.6The oscilloscope

An draws a graph of voltage against time. The screen is divided into a grid — the — usually 10 divisions across and 8 high. You do not read volts off the screen directly. You count divisions and multiply by the setting of a knob.

volts = divisions × volts/div
seconds = divisions × time/div

How to set it up, every time

  1. Set coupling to DC unless you specifically want to hide an offset.
  2. Adjust volts/div until the waveform fills most of the screen height without clipping. A trace using two divisions is far harder to read accurately than one using six.
  3. Adjust time/div until you see two or three complete cycles. One cycle makes the period hard to judge; twenty makes it impossible.
  4. Count divisions, then multiply. Do not estimate volts by eye.

The probe multiplier

Most scope probes have a switch marked 1× and 10×. On 10× the probe divides the signal by ten before it reaches the scope, which improves the measurement but means every voltage you read must be multiplied by ten — unless the scope has been told which probe is fitted. Check the probe switch and the scope's probe setting agree before you trust a number. A reading off by exactly ten is almost always this.

Guided problem 7 · reading amplitude off the screen

The oscilloscope below is set as marked. What is the peak-to-peak voltage of the displayed signal?

  • Count divisions from the lowest point of the trace to the highest, then multiply by the volts per division setting.
  • The trace spans six divisions vertically. The vertical control is at 0.5 V per division.
  1. Count vertically: the trace runs 3 divisions above the centre line and 3 below, so 6 divisions peak-to-peak.
  2. The vertical setting is 0.5 V per division.
  3. Volts = divisions × volts/div = 6 × 0.5.
  4. The signal is 3.0 V peak-to-peak. Its RMS value would be 0.707 × 1.5 = 1.06 V, since the peak is half the peak-to-peak.

Guided problem 8 · reading frequency off the screen

A different signal is shown below, with the controls set as marked. What is its frequency in hertz?

  • Find the period first by counting the divisions in one complete cycle, then take the reciprocal.
  • One full cycle spans five divisions and the timebase is at 50 µs per division.
  1. Count one complete cycle: it occupies 5 divisions.
  2. Period = divisions × time/div = 5 × 50 µs = 250 µs.
  3. Convert to seconds: 250 µs = 0.000 250 s.
  4. f = 1 ÷ T = 1 ÷ 0.000250 = 4 000 Hz, or 4 kHz.

A note on searching for scope help

Most oscilloscope tutorials online are aimed at digital storage scopes with automatic measurement buttons. Those buttons are genuinely useful, but learn to count divisions first. When a reading looks wrong, division counting is how you find out whether the signal or the setting is at fault.
