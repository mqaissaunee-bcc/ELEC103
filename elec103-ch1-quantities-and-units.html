The one skill that carries the whole course

Handling very large and very small numbers without losing track of scale. A resistor is 4 700 ohms; a current is 0.000 25 amperes; a capacitor is 0.000 000 1 farads. Writing those out is unworkable, so electronics has its own way of expressing them. That is what sections 1.3 through 1.5 are about, and every later chapter assumes you have it.

1.2Units and quantities

Every measurement has two parts: a number and a unit. Drop the unit and the number means nothing. This is not pedantry — it is the single most common way to lose points on an exam and the most common way to blow a fuse in lab.

The SI base units

Seven units are defined independently; everything else is from them. Only four come up regularly in this course.

Current is the odd one out

Of all the electrical quantities, only current is an . Everything else — voltage, resistance, power, charge — is derived from it and from the mechanical base units. One ampere is one coulomb per second, which makes current the bridge between electricity and the rest of physics.

Writing units correctly

  • A unit named after a person is capitalized as a symbol but not as a word: 12 volts, written 12 V. Ampere, ohm, watt, and volt all follow this rule.
  • Leave a space between the number and the unit: 12 V, not 12V.
  • Units are never pluralized as symbols. Twelve volts is 12 V, never 12 Vs.

Magnetic units, for later

The book lists a table of magnetic units — weber, tesla, ampere-turn. You will not need them in this course. They arrive in the second course with magnetism and inductors. Recognize them when you see them and move on.

1.3Scientific and engineering notation

Both notations write a number as a coefficient times a power of ten. They differ in one rule, and that difference is the reason electronics uses one and not the other.

  • Scientific notation puts exactly one digit before the decimal point. The exponent can be anything.
  • Engineering notation allows one, two, or three digits before the decimal point, and requires the exponent to be a multiple of three.

The multiple-of-three rule is what makes engineering notation useful: every exponent that is a multiple of three has a metric prefix attached to it. Write 47 × 103 and you can read it straight off as 47 kilohms.

Reading the exponent

A positive exponent counts how many places the decimal point moves right; a negative exponent counts how many places it moves left. That is the whole mechanism.

Worked example 1

Write 4.7 × 106 as an ordinary decimal number.

The exponent is +6, so the point moves 6 places right. 4.7 → 4 700 000 4 700 000

Worked example 2

Write 27 × 10−6 as an ordinary decimal number.

The exponent is −6, so the point moves 6 places left. 27. → 0.000 027 0.000 027

Your calculator does this for you

Every scientific calculator can display in normal, scientific, or engineering mode. On a TI-84 the choice is under the MODE menu; pick ENG and the calculator will report every answer with an exponent that is a multiple of three. Set it once at the start of the semester and leave it. Use the EE or ×10x key to enter exponents rather than typing "times ten to the" by hand, which introduces errors.

Guided problem 1 · engineering notation

A current is measured as 0.000 047 A. Express it in engineering notation, then state the value with its metric prefix. Enter just the coefficient.

  • needs an exponent that is a multiple of three. Which multiples of three could work here?
  • Move the decimal point until one, two or three digits sit in front of it. Count the places as you go.
  1. Start at 0.000 047 A. The point has to move right to make the number bigger than one.
  2. It moves 6 places: 0.000 047 → 47. Six is a multiple of three, so this is a valid engineering exponent.
  3. The number is therefore 47 × 10⁻⁶ A.
  4. 10⁻⁶ is micro, so the reading is 47 µA.

1.4Metric prefixes

A is a name for a power of ten. Once a number is in engineering notation, the prefix is just a substitution: 103 becomes k, 10−3 becomes m, and the number reads as a quantity a technician would say out loud.

Prefix ladder

the same quantity, every way of writing it

    The highlighted row is the one an engineer would write: a coefficient between 1 and 999.

    The prefixes worth memorizing

    Six of them cover essentially everything in this course. Learn these cold and look the rest up.

    Note the capitalization rule: prefixes for positive exponents are capitalized from mega upward, while kilo and every negative prefix are lowercase. This is not decorative. M is mega and m is milli — a factor of one billion apart.

    The mistake that costs the most marks

    Writing 4.7 MΩ when you meant 4.7 mΩ. One is a resistor you would find in a voltmeter input; the other is closer to a piece of wire. Capitalization in this subject carries meaning, and no partial credit survives getting it backwards.

    Converting between prefixes

    Moving from a larger unit to a smaller one makes the number bigger, so the decimal point moves right. Moving from smaller to larger makes the number smaller, so the point moves left. If your converted number looks wrong by a factor of a thousand, you moved it the wrong way.

    Worked example 3

    Convert 2.7 mA to amperes.

    Milli is 10⁻³, and we are moving to a larger unit. 2.7 mA = 2.7 × 10⁻³ A 0.0027 A

    Guided problem 2 · reading a prefixed value

    The meter below is measuring a resistor. Express that resistance in ohms.

    • The k on the display is a prefix, not part of the number.
    • Moving from a larger unit to a smaller one makes the number bigger, so the decimal point moves right.
    1. The display reads 4.70 kΩ, which means 4.70 × 10³ Ω.
    2. Kilo is a factor of 1 000, so move the decimal point three places right.
    3. 4.70 → 4 700.
    4. The resistance is 4 700 Ω. Note the meter kept three significant figures, so it is 4 700 Ω and not merely "about 5 k".

    1.5Arithmetic with prefixes

    The rule for adding and subtracting is short: convert both numbers to the same prefix first, then add. There is no way around it, and skipping it is how 10 000 Ω + 22 kΩ becomes a wrong answer.

    Worked example 4

    Add 10 000 Ω and 22 kΩ. Give your answer in kilohms.

    Convert to a common prefix: 10 000 Ω = 10 kΩ 10 kΩ + 22 kΩ 32 kΩ

    Multiplying and dividing is different

    Here the prefixes do not have to match, because the exponents simply add or subtract. This is what makes some combinations so convenient that they are worth memorizing:

    • volts ÷ kilohms gives milliamperes
    • milliamperes × kilohms gives volts
    • volts ÷ milliamperes gives kilohms

    Those three shortcuts remove nearly all prefix conversion from Chapters 3 through 7. They are worth committing to memory now.

    Guided problem 3 · arithmetic across prefixes

    Add 470 Ω, 1.2 kΩ and 0.0022 MΩ. Give the total in kilohms.

    • These three values are in three different prefixes. You cannot add them as they stand.
    • The answer is asked for in kilohms, so convert everything to kilohms first.
    1. 470 Ω is smaller than a kilohm, so the number gets smaller: 470 ÷ 1 000 = 0.47 kΩ.
    2. 1.2 kΩ is already in kilohms, so it stays as it is.
    3. 0.0022 MΩ is larger than a kilohm, so the number gets bigger: 0.0022 × 1 000 = 2.2 kΩ.
    4. Now they can be added: 0.47 + 1.2 + 2.2 = 3.87 kΩ.

    1.6Error, accuracy, and precision

    Every measurement is uncertain. That is not a failure of technique; it is a property of measurement itself. What separates a good technician from a careless one is knowing how uncertain a reading is and saying so.

    Three words get used loosely in ordinary speech and mean specific things here.

    • is the difference between the measured value and the true or accepted value.
    • describes how close a measurement lands to the true value.
    • describes how repeatable a measurement is, whether or not it is correct.

    A meter can be precise and wrong. If it reads 9.72 V every single time on a source that is genuinely 9.00 V, it is highly precise and badly inaccurate. That combination is the dangerous one, because repeatability feels like correctness.

    Accuracy against precision

    Where the uncertainty comes from

    Three sources account for most of it in this lab, and a good report names which one dominates.

    • The instrument. A meter is specified to a , often something like 0.5% of reading plus one digit. That last digit is never trustworthy.
    • The component. A 5% resistor marked 1.0 kΩ may legitimately measure 950 Ω.
    • The method. Inserting a meter changes the circuit slightly, and reading an analog scale from an angle introduces parallax.

    What this means for your lab reports

    Never write that a measurement "matched" the calculation. Write the calculated value, the measured value, the , and one sentence on which source of uncertainty accounts for it. That sentence is what the Interpret Data outcome is asking for, and it is what separates a B report from an A.

    Guided problem 4 · percent difference

    A circuit is calculated to draw 12.0 mA. The meter reads 11.6 mA. What is the percent difference between the measurement and the calculation?

    • The calculated value is the reference — it goes on the bottom of the fraction.
    • Use the size of the difference. A percent difference is quoted as a positive number.
    1. Find the difference: |11.6 − 12.0| = 0.4 mA.
    2. Divide by the calculated value: 0.4 ÷ 12.0 = 0.0333.
    3. Multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage.
    4. The percent difference is 3.33%, which is well inside what a 5% resistor and an ordinary meter can account for.

    1.7Significant figures

    are how you tell a reader how much of your number you actually believe. Reporting 2.7381 V from a meter that resolves to hundredths is a claim you cannot support.

    The rules

    1. Every non-zero digit is significant.
    2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant. 1002 has four.
    3. Leading zeros are never significant. They only place the decimal point: 0.0025 has two.
    4. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant. 2.50 has three, and that final zero is a claim about precision.
    5. Trailing zeros in a whole number with no decimal point are ambiguous. This is why 1200 is better written in engineering notation, where the ambiguity disappears.

    Significant figure inspector

    Carrying them through a calculation

    When you multiply or divide, the answer gets the same number of significant figures as the least precise input. When you add or subtract, the answer gets the same number of decimal places as the least precise input.

    Do the rounding once, at the end. Rounding at every intermediate step accumulates its own error, which is exactly what significant figures exist to prevent.

    Worked example 5

    A voltage of 12.5 V is measured across a resistance of 1 000 Ω known to four significant figures. Report the current with the right number of significant figures.

    12.5 V ÷ 1 000 Ω = 0.0125 A The least precise input, 12.5 V, has three significant figures. 0.0125 A, or 12.5 mA

    Guided problem 5 · significant figures in a result

    A resistance is marked 4.7 kΩ, known to two significant figures. A current of 2.15 mA is measured to three. The voltage is the product of the two. Report the voltage with the correct number of significant figures.

    • Do the arithmetic first, then decide how much of the answer you are entitled to keep.
    • For multiplication, the answer takes the same number of significant figures as the least precise input.
    1. Multiply: 2.15 mA × 4.7 kΩ = 10.105 V. Milliamperes times kilohms gives volts directly.
    2. Count the significant figures in each input: 4.7 has two, 2.15 has three.
    3. The least precise input has two, so the answer may carry only two.
    4. Rounded to two significant figures, the voltage is 10 V. Reporting 10.105 V would claim a precision the 4.7 kΩ marking does not support.

    1.8Collecting and graphing data

    Half of this course is lab work, and a lab report is mostly a graph with an argument attached. This section is about making the graph say something true.

    Recording as you go

    Write down every reading when you take it, in ink, with its units, before you do any arithmetic. Record what the meter actually showed rather than what you expected. A reading that surprises you is data; a reading you quietly adjusted is not.

    Which quantity goes on which axis

    The quantity you control goes on the horizontal axis; the quantity you measure goes on the vertical. If you set the supply voltage and read the resulting current, voltage is horizontal and current is vertical. Say it as "current against voltage" and the order is already right.

    Choosing the scale

    Pick a scale where the data fills most of the plot. A graph where every point huddles in one corner wastes the page and hides the trend. But when your values span more than about two powers of ten, a stops working, and a is the honest choice.

    Same data, two scales

    Drawing the line

    If the relationship should be a straight line, draw that passes as near as possible to all the points. Do not connect the dots. Connecting the dots claims that every wiggle between measurements is real, when almost always it is measurement error.

    Points that sit far off the line are worth a sentence in your report. Sometimes they are mistakes worth repeating; sometimes they are the most interesting thing you found.

    The Excel requirement

    Assignment 4 must be produced in Microsoft Excel to be eligible for an A. Use a scatter plot with markers, not a line chart — a line chart treats your x-values as evenly spaced labels rather than as numbers, which silently distorts the shape. Add a linear trendline and display its equation; the slope of that line is usually the quantity the lab is actually asking you to find.

    What every graph needs

    • A title saying what is plotted against what
    • Both axes labeled with a quantity and its unit
    • A scale with round, evenly spaced intervals
    • Plotted points visible as points, not hidden under a line

    Guided problem 6 · reading a value off a graph

    The graph shows current measured through one resistor as the voltage was raised. Use the graph to find the resistance in ohms.

    • Pick two points that are far apart on the line. Points close together make the slope hard to read accurately.
    • The slope of a current-against-voltage line is 1/R, not R. You will need to invert it at the end.
    1. Take the origin and the far end of the line: at 0 V the current is 0 mA, and at 10 V it is 20 mA.
    2. Slope = rise ÷ run = 20 mA ÷ 10 V = 2 mA per volt.
    3. In base units that is 0.002 A per volt.
    4. The slope is 1/R, so R = 1 ÷ 0.002 = 500 Ω.
