6.1Recognizing a parallel connection

Components are when both ends of each one connect to the same two points. Current arriving at that first point has a choice of routes, and every route ends up back at the second point.

Those two points are called . If you can name two nodes and every component you are considering runs directly between them, they are in parallel — no matter how the schematic is drawn.

The two tests, side by side

Series: trace from one end to the other without ever choosing a route. Parallel: both ends of every component land on the same two nodes. If neither test passes, you have a series-parallel combination, which is Chapter 7.

Drawings lie about topology

Parallel components are usually drawn as vertical branches side by side, but they do not have to be. Two resistors on opposite sides of a schematic are in parallel if their ends meet at the same nodes. Follow the wire, not the picture. A wire with no component in it is a single point electrically, however long it is drawn.

6.2Voltage is common

In a parallel circuit the voltage across every branch is the same. It has to be: each branch connects to the same two nodes, and the voltage between two points is one number.

This is the exact mirror of the series rule. In series the current was common and the voltage divided. In parallel the voltage is common and the current divides. Nearly everything else in this chapter follows from that swap.

Why the lights in your house are in parallel

Every outlet and fixture sits across the same two conductors, so each one gets the full supply voltage no matter what else is switched on. Wire them in series instead and they would divide the voltage between them, dimming as you added more, and one failure would take out the lot.

Finding branch currents

Because each branch has the full source voltage across it, each branch current comes straight from Ohm's law applied to that branch alone:

Ix = VS / Rx

No branch cares what the others are doing. Add another branch and the existing branch currents do not change at all — only the total drawn from the source goes up.

Worked example 1

A 12 V source is across a 1.0 kΩ resistor and a 2.2 kΩ resistor in parallel. Find the current in the 1.0 kΩ branch.

Each branch has the full 12 V across it. I1 = VS / R1 = 12 V ÷ 1 000 Ω I1 = 12 mA

The 2.2 kΩ branch is irrelevant to this answer. It draws its own 5.45 mA independently.

6.3Kirchhoff's current law

states that the total current into a node equals the total current out of it. Equivalently, the algebraic sum of the currents at any node is zero.

IT = I1 + I2 + I3 + …

Like KVL, it is a conservation statement. Charge does not pile up at a junction and it does not vanish there. Whatever arrives must leave.

Current at a node

Worked example 2

A source supplies 25 mA to three parallel branches. Two of them carry 10 mA and 7 mA. What does the third carry?

IT = I1 + I2 + I3 25 = 10 + 7 + I3 I3 = 8 mA

The pair you will use for the rest of the course

KVL is about a loop and voltages; KCL is about a node and currents. Together they solve any resistive circuit, including ones too tangled to reduce by inspection. Chapter 7 uses both on the same circuit, and the second course builds entire analysis methods on them.

6.4Total resistance falls

Adding a resistor in parallel gives current another route, so the total opposition goes down. The general formula uses reciprocals:

1 / RT = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + …

The step everyone forgets is the last one. That expression gives you one over the total, so you must invert the sum at the end. An answer that comes out larger than your smallest resistor is the signature of this mistake.

The rule that catches the error

The total resistance of a parallel combination is always smaller than the smallest branch. Two 1 kΩ resistors in parallel are 500 Ω. A 1 kΩ in parallel with a 1 MΩ is about 999 Ω — nearly the smaller one, barely changed by the big one. If your answer is not below the smallest branch, you have made an error.

6.5Two shortcuts worth memorizing

Equal branches

If every branch is the same value, the total is that value divided by how many branches there are:

RT = R / n

Four 1 kΩ resistors in parallel are 250 Ω. This comes up constantly, because equal resistors in parallel is how you build a resistance you do not have in the drawer, and how you spread power across several parts.

Exactly two branches

For two resistors, the reciprocal formula collapses into the rule:

RT = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2)

It only works for two at a time. For three resistors you can apply it twice — combine two, then combine that result with the third — or use the reciprocal formula directly.

Worked example 3

Find the total resistance of 1.0 kΩ and 2.2 kΩ in parallel.

RT = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2) RT = (1 000 × 2 200) / (1 000 + 2 200) RT = 2 200 000 / 3 200 RT = 687.5 Ω

Below 1 000 Ω, as it must be.

6.6The current divider rule

Just as a series circuit divides voltage in proportion to resistance, a parallel circuit divides current in inverse proportion to it:

Ix = (RT / Rx) × IT

The smallest resistance takes the largest current, because it offers the easiest route. That inversion is the single most common source of error in this chapter — the voltage divider puts Rx on top, and the current divider puts it underneath.

The two-branch shortcut

For exactly two branches there is a neater form: the current in one branch is the total multiplied by the other branch's resistance over the sum. I₁ = R₂/(R₁+R₂) × Iₜ. Note it is the opposite resistor on top, which is the same inversion stated a second way.

6.7Current sources in parallel

A delivers a set current regardless of what resistance it is connected to, in the same way a voltage source holds a set voltage. It is the dual of everything you have learned so far.

Current sources in parallel add, with polarity respected, exactly as voltage sources in series did. Two 10 mA sources pointing the same way give 20 mA; reverse one and they cancel to zero.

Where you will actually meet one

Rarely as a physical component in this course, and constantly as a model. A transistor behaves approximately like a current source, and the second course uses current sources heavily when converting between circuit models. For now it is enough to know the rule and recognize the symbol: a circle with an arrow, as against the two parallel lines of a voltage source.

Why they are never in series

Two voltage sources in parallel with different voltages is a contradiction — the same two nodes cannot be at two different voltages. Two current sources in series with different currents is the same contradiction in the mirror: the same single path cannot carry two different currents. In practice, connecting either is a fault, not a design.

6.8Power in a parallel circuit

Total power is the sum of the branch powers, exactly as it was in series:

PT = P1 + P2 + P3 + …

What changes is which resistor gets hottest. In series, the current was common, so the largest resistance dissipated the most. In parallel, the voltage is common, so P = V²/R is the convenient form — and the smallest resistance dissipates the most.

This reverses the intuition you just built

Students who learned in Chapter 5 that the biggest resistor runs hottest carry that into Chapter 6 and get it exactly backwards. Tie the rule to the common quantity rather than memorizing it: whichever quantity is shared, the other one determines who works hardest.

Worked example 4

A 12 V source is across 1.0 kΩ, 2.2 kΩ, and 3.3 kΩ in parallel. Find the total power in milliwatts.

Each branch: P = V²/R 144/1 000 = 144 mW, 144/2 200 = 65.5 mW, 144/3 300 = 43.6 mW PT = 253 mW

Check it from the total: RT = 569 Ω, so PT = 144 ÷ 569 = 253 mW. The two routes agree exactly, which is the point of doing both.

6.9Faults are harder to see here

A series fault stops everything, which makes it obvious. A parallel fault often does not, which makes it dangerous.

An open branch

One branch goes open. That branch's current drops to zero. Every other branch is completely unaffected — same voltage, same current — because they never depended on it. The total current falls by exactly the missing branch's share, and the circuit carries on working.

So a voltmeter tells you nothing. Every branch still reads the full source voltage, including the open one. You have to measure current, or lift the component and measure its resistance out of circuit.

A shorted branch

Now the opposite. A short across one branch is a short across all of them, because they share the same two nodes. Total resistance collapses toward zero, current spikes, and the supply either current-limits, blows a fuse, or damages something. Every branch reads zero volts.

Why this matters beyond the exam

A string of parallel lamps with one open looks almost normal — slightly less current drawn, everything else lit. A failed branch in a real system can sit undetected for a long time, which is why equipment that matters monitors current rather than voltage. Voltage tells you the supply is present; current tells you the load is actually there.

6.10Series and parallel, side by side

Every rule in this chapter is the mirror of one in Chapter 5. Learning them as a pair is far more reliable than learning them as two lists, because you can reconstruct either side from the question "which quantity is common here?"

Same three resistors, two ways

The mirror, stated once

  • Series: one path → current common → voltage divides → resistances add → total larger than the largest.
  • Parallel: two nodes → voltage common → current divides → resistances combine reciprocally → total smaller than the smallest.

The fault behavior mirrors too. An open in series stops everything and is obvious; an open in parallel affects one branch and hides. A short in series bypasses one component; a short in parallel bypasses the whole circuit.

Chapter 7 in one sentence

Real circuits contain both, and the work is deciding which rule applies to which part before you calculate anything. If you can look at three resistors and say confidently which two are in parallel with each other, Chapter 7 is mostly bookkeeping.

A note on the one rule that reverses

In series the largest resistor runs hottest; in parallel the smallest does. Do not memorize both. Ask which quantity is common: if the current is shared, power follows I²R and rises with R. If the voltage is shared, power follows V²/R and rises as R falls.

The four rules

Pathsone per branch, between two nodes
Voltagethe same across every branch
Currentdivides; branch currents sum to the total
Resistance1/Rₜ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …

Rₜ is always below the smallest branch. If it is not, you forgot to invert.

Two shortcuts

Equal branchesRₜ = R / n
Exactly twoRₜ = (R₁R₂) / (R₁+R₂)

Product over sum handles only two at a time. For three, apply it twice or use reciprocals.

Working a parallel circuit

  • Every branch has the full source voltage.
  • Each branch current: I = Vₛ ÷ R for that branch.
  • Total current: add the branch currents.
  • Or: Rₜ first, then Iₜ = Vₛ ÷ Rₜ. Both must agree.

Current divider rule

Iₓ = (Rₜ / Rₓ) × Iₜ

For two branches: I₁ = R₂/(R₁+R₂) × Iₜ — the other resistor goes on top. Smallest resistance takes the largest current.

Kirchhoff's current law

Iₜ = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ + …

Current in equals current out at any node. Use it as a check, and to find the last unknown branch current.

Power

Each branchP = V²/R, with V common
Totalthe sum, or Vₛ × Iₜ
Hottest partthe smallest resistance

This reverses the series rule. Tie it to which quantity is common.

Fault signatures

One branch draws 0 mA, voltage normal everywherethat branch is open
Voltage collapses, current over rangea branch is shorted
Branch currents sum to the totalno fault

A voltmeter cannot find an open branch. Measure current.
