4.1Energy and power are not the same thing

People use the two words interchangeably in ordinary speech. In this course they are separate quantities with separate units, and confusing them is the source of most of the errors in this chapter.

  • is the capacity to do work, measured in joules.
  • is the rate at which energy is used, measured in watts.

P = W / t

One watt is one joule per second. A 60 W lamp does not contain 60 of anything; it consumes 60 joules every second it is on. Leave it on longer and it uses more energy at the same power.

Worked example 1

A component uses 1 500 J of energy in 15 seconds. What is its power?

P = W / t P = 1 500 J ÷ 15 s P = 100 W

The kilowatt-hour

Your electric bill is not in joules. It is in , which is a unit of energy built from a power multiplied by a time: one kilowatt sustained for one hour. It is a large unit — 3.6 million joules — which is exactly why the utility uses it.

Why this matters at the bench

The same distinction decides whether a resistor survives. A resistor can pass a large current briefly without harm; sustain it and the resistor cooks. Power tells you the rate at which energy is arriving as heat, and section 4.4 turns that into a component you can actually buy.

4.2Three formulas, one idea

Power in an electrical component is voltage across it multiplied by current through it:

P = V × I

That is the only power formula you strictly need. The other two exist because you often know a resistance rather than one of these two quantities, and Ohm's law lets you substitute.

Substitute V = IR into P = VI and you get P = I²R. Substitute I = V/R instead and you get P = V²/R. Same relationship, three faces.

Which formula do I use?

Pick the two quantities you already know.

Pick two

Any two of voltage, current, and resistance are enough to find the power. Which formula you reach for depends only on which two you have.

Watch the squares

Two of the three formulas square a quantity, and that changes the arithmetic in a way people underestimate. Doubling the current through a fixed resistor does not double the power — it quadruples it. That single fact is why an overloaded circuit fails so suddenly rather than gradually.

Square the quantity, not the whole thing

In P = I²R, only the current is squared. A frequent error is computing (IR)² instead, which is a different number entirely. On your calculator, square the current first, then multiply by the resistance.

4.3Calculating power

Worked example 2

A resistor has 12 V across it and 50 mA through it. What power does it dissipate?

P = V × I P = 12 V × 0.050 A P = 0.60 W = 600 mW

Worked example 3

A current of 4.7 mA is in a 4.7 kΩ resistor. Find the power.

P = I² × R P = (0.0047 A)² × 4 700 Ω P = 0.000 022 09 × 4 700 P = 0.1038 W ≈ 104 mW

A quarter-watt resistor handles this comfortably. An eighth-watt part would be running at 83% of its rating, which section 4.4 explains is too close.

Worked example 4

A 100 Ω resistor has 12 V across it. Find the power without calculating the current first.

P = V² / R P = (12 V)² ÷ 100 Ω P = 144 ÷ 100 P = 1.44 W

4.4Power ratings and choosing a resistor

A resistor has two specifications, not one. The resistance says how much it opposes current. The says how much heat it can shed before it damages itself. Buy the right ohms with the wrong watts and the circuit works for a few minutes.

Standard ratings for the parts on your bench are 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 1 and 2 watts. Physically larger means a greater surface area, which means more heat shed, which is why you can often estimate a resistor's rating by looking at it.

The rule of thumb

Choose a resistor rated for at least twice the power it will actually dissipate. That factor of two is not superstition. It covers the tolerance on the resistor, the tolerance on the supply, still air inside an enclosure, and the fact that a component run at its limit ages faster than one run gently.

So a resistor dissipating 104 mW wants a quarter-watt part, not an eighth. One dissipating 300 mW wants a one-watt part, because half a watt leaves only a 1.67 safety factor.

Ratings assume the part can breathe

The number on the datasheet is measured in free air at room temperature. Pack a resistor inside a sealed box next to three others and its real capacity is lower. Manufacturers publish derating curves for exactly this reason. In this lab, on an open breadboard, the free-air figure is fair — but do not carry that assumption into a product.

4.5How resistors fail

A resistor asked to dissipate more power than it can shed gets hot, and the heat does the damage. What you find afterwards depends on how far past the limit it went.

The progression

  1. Running warm. Above about half its rating, a resistor is noticeably warm to a cautious finger. Not a fault, but a sign that the design has no margin.
  2. Discoloration. The body darkens and the color bands scorch. The resistance has usually drifted upward and out of tolerance by this point, permanently.
  3. Open circuit. The film or wire inside breaks. The resistor now measures infinite resistance, and the circuit stops. This is the most common end state.

An overheated resistor almost always fails open, not short. That is deliberate and it is a good thing: an open component removes itself from the circuit, while a short would keep drawing current and take other parts with it.

Finding one

A burned resistor is often visible, and the smell is unmistakable and hard to forget. But do not trust your eyes alone — a film resistor can fail open with no visible mark at all. Measure it. Out of circuit, an open resistor reads OL or infinity on the ohmmeter. In circuit, apply what Chapter 3 taught: in a series string, an open component has the entire source voltage across it while the good ones read zero.

The failure tells you where to look

A resistor does not overheat on its own. Something made it dissipate more power than the design intended: a supply set too high, a wiring error putting it across the full source, or another component that shorted and raised the current. Replace the burned resistor without finding that cause and you will replace it again.

Worked example 5

A 100 Ω quarter-watt resistor is accidentally connected directly across a 12 V supply, with nothing else in the circuit. How much power does it try to dissipate?

P = V² / R P = 144 ÷ 100 P = 1.44 W — nearly six times its rating

It will not survive this for long. This exact mistake, a resistor placed across the supply instead of in series with the load, is the most common way a part gets destroyed in this lab.

4.6How long will the battery last?

A battery's capacity is given as an rating: the product of a current and the time it can sustain it. A 100 Ah battery can in principle supply 1 A for 100 hours, or 10 A for 10 hours, or 100 A for one hour.

time = ampere-hours ÷ current

Where the arithmetic lies to you

The relationship is only approximately true, and it breaks down at high currents. Draw a battery down fast and you get noticeably less total energy out of it than the rating promises. Manufacturers quote capacity at a specified discharge rate for that reason. Treat the calculation as an upper bound, not a guarantee.

Ampere-hours are not energy

An ampere-hour is a quantity of charge, not of energy. To get energy you have to multiply by the voltage: a 2.5 Ah battery at 3.7 V holds about 9.25 watt-hours, while the same 2.5 Ah at 12 V holds 30 Wh. This is why comparing two batteries on ampere-hours alone tells you nothing unless they are the same voltage.

4.7Power supply efficiency

No supply delivers everything it takes in. is the fraction that reaches the output:

efficiency = Pout ÷ Pin × 100%

The difference between input and output does not vanish. It becomes heat inside the supply, which is why a power brick is warm and why anything above a few hundred watts has a fan.

Where the power goes

Reading it the other way

Efficiency is usually quoted as a percentage, but the number that matters thermally is the waste. A supply at 90% efficient delivering 100 W is producing 11 W of heat inside its own case. Push the same supply to 95% efficient and the heat drops to about 5 W — half as much. Small changes in efficiency make large changes in cooling.

Worked example 6

A supply draws 25 W from the wall and delivers 20 W to the load. What is its efficiency?

efficiency = Pout / Pin × 100 = 20 W ÷ 25 W × 100 = 80%, with 5 W lost as heat

A note on the squared terms

If one thing from this chapter survives to the final exam, make it this: power depends on the square of the current or the voltage. Halving a current cuts the heat to a quarter. That single relationship explains fuse ratings, wire gauges, heat sinks, and why transmission lines run at high voltage.
