Two patterns should show up as you move the sliders. Raise the voltage and the current rises with it. Raise the resistance and the current falls. Georg Ohm published that relationship in 1827, and it holds for every resistive circuit you will build in this course.

Where the water picture helps, and where it lies

Pressure pushing water through a narrow pipe is a fair picture of voltage pushing charge through a resistor. It breaks down at a cut wire. Cut a water pipe and water sprays out. Cut a circuit and nothing spills — the current simply stops everywhere at once, including in the parts of the circuit far from the break. Charge needs a complete loop, and water does not.

What this section asks of you

By the end of the chapter you should be able to state Ohm's law in all three of its forms, calculate any one of the three quantities when you know the other two, read the two graphs that describe the relationship, and use it to find a fault in a circuit that is not behaving.

3.2The three quantities, briefly

Chapter 2 introduced these separately. Ohm's law is where they meet, so it is worth pinning down what each one actually measures before combining them.

Voltage is the push

is energy per unit of charge, measured in volts. A 12 V battery gives every coulomb of charge 12 joules of energy to spend on its way around the circuit. Voltage is always measured between two points — this is why you place a voltmeter across a component rather than in line with it. A single point does not have a voltage any more than a single step has a height difference.

Current is the flow

is charge per unit of time, measured in amperes. One ampere is one coulomb passing a point each second. Current is measured through a component, which is why an ammeter has to be inserted into the path, with the circuit broken open to receive it.

Resistance is the opposition

is the opposition to current, measured in ohms. In this course resistance mostly comes from resistors you choose deliberately, but every wire, every switch contact, and every meter lead has some of it too.

A habit worth forming now

Before you calculate anything, say out loud which quantity you are after and which two you already know. Most wrong answers in this chapter are not arithmetic errors. They are cases of dividing the wrong pair of numbers.

Direction of current

Your textbook uses , which treats current as flowing from the positive terminal of the source, around the circuit, to the negative terminal. Electrons in a metal actually drift the other way. Both conventions give identical answers for everything in this course, so use the one the book and your instructor use, and be aware the other exists when you read outside sources.

3.3Ohm's law and its three forms

states that current is to voltage and to resistance. In symbols:

I = V / R

Directly proportional means that doubling the voltage doubles the current, as long as resistance stays put. Inversely proportional means that doubling the resistance halves the current, as long as voltage stays put. Those two sentences are the entire law. The other two forms are the same equation solved for a different letter.

Cover the quantity you want

Pick a letter

Cover the quantity you are solving for. What is left in the triangle is the formula: side by side means multiply, one over the other means divide.

The triangle is a crutch, not an explanation

It is a fine memory aid for your first few weeks, and useless the moment a problem has more than three quantities in it. Practice rearranging I = V/R algebraically as well, because chapters 5 through 7 will stack several of these relationships together and no triangle covers that.

Units must match the formula

Ohm's law is written in base units: volts, amperes, ohms. If you feed it kilohms without converting, the answer comes out in milliamperes rather than amperes, which is convenient once you know it and a trap until then. Section 3.6 works through this in detail.

3.4Calculating current

Current is the quantity you will calculate most often, because it is the one that is most awkward to measure. Measuring voltage means touching two probes to a live circuit. Measuring current means breaking the circuit open and inserting the meter into the path. Given the choice, a technician measures voltage and calculates current.

Worked example 1

A 100 Ω resistor is connected across a 12 V source. How much current is in the circuit?

I = V / R I = 12 V / 100 Ω I = 0.12 A = 120 mA

Worked example 2

The same 12 V source is connected across a 2.2 kΩ resistor instead. Find the current.

I = V / R I = 12 V / 2 200 Ω I = 0.005 45 A = 5.45 mA

Twenty-two times the resistance, one twenty-second of the current. Inverse proportion in action.

3.5Calculating voltage and resistance

Finding voltage: V = IR

This form answers the question every series circuit will eventually ask you: how much voltage is dropped across this particular resistor? Multiply the current through it by its resistance.

Worked example 3

A current of 2.5 mA is in a 4.7 kΩ resistor. What is the voltage across it?

V = I × R V = 0.0025 A × 4 700 Ω V = 11.75 V ≈ 11.8 V

Shortcut worth memorizing: milliamperes times kilohms gives volts directly. 2.5 mA × 4.7 kΩ = 11.75 V, with no conversion needed.

Finding resistance: R = V/I

You will use this one less often for design and more often for diagnosis. If a component is drawing more current than it should at a known voltage, its resistance has dropped, and that usually means something inside it has failed.

Worked example 4

A lamp draws 830 mA from a 6.0 V supply. What is its resistance while lit?

R = V / I R = 6.0 V / 0.830 A R = 7.23 Ω

A lamp is not a resistor

Measure that same lamp cold with an ohmmeter and you will get a much smaller number, often under one ohm. The tungsten filament's resistance climbs steeply as it heats. Ohm's law still holds at every instant, but the resistance is not a constant, so the lamp is a non-linear device. Section 3.6 shows what that does to the graph.

3.6Seeing the law as a graph

Two graphs describe Ohm's law, and they look nothing alike. Which one you get depends on which quantity you hold constant.

Graph explorer

Current versus voltage is a straight line

Hold the resistance fixed, sweep the voltage, and plot the current. You get a straight line through the origin. A relationship that graphs as a straight line is called linear, and this is exactly what people mean when they call a resistor a linear component.

The carries information. Keep several curves on the plot at once and you will see it: the smaller the resistance, the steeper the line. Slope is I/V, which is 1/R. A steep line is a small resistor letting a lot of current through for each additional volt.

The line also passes through the origin, and that matters. Zero volts gives zero current. There is no threshold to overcome and no offset, which is another way of saying a resistor does not care which direction you push it.

Current versus resistance is a curve

Now hold the voltage fixed and sweep the resistance instead. The plot drops steeply at first, then flattens into a long tail that approaches but never reaches zero. This shape is a hyperbola, and it is the signature of an inverse relationship.

The practical lesson is in the shape's asymmetry. Going from 100 Ω to 200 Ω costs you a lot of current. Going from 9 kΩ to 10 kΩ barely changes anything. When you are choosing a resistor to set a current, small values need far more precision than large ones.

What a non-linear device looks like

Plot current against voltage for the lamp from section 3.5 and you will not get a straight line. It bends over as the voltage climbs, because the filament heats up and its resistance rises. Diodes, transistors, and thermistors all bend in their own characteristic ways. Reading the shape of an I–V curve is how you identify a device you cannot see.

3.7At the bench

Everything so far has been calculation. The lab that goes with this chapter asks you to build the circuit and prove the numbers with a meter. This section is what to expect before you get there.

What you will build

A single resistor across a DC supply, on a breadboard, with the supply set to a voltage your instructor specifies. You will measure the voltage across the resistor and the current through it, calculate the third quantity, and compare all three against the resistor's marked value.

How each measurement connects

This is the part that costs people fuses and lab time, so learn it before you sit down.

  • Voltage. Probes touch two points, one on each side of the component. The circuit stays whole. The meter has very high internal resistance, so almost no current diverts into it.
  • Current. The circuit must be broken and the meter inserted so that all the current passes through it. The meter has very low internal resistance. Placing an ammeter across a supply the way you would a voltmeter is a short circuit, and that is how meter fuses die.
  • Resistance. Power off, and the component lifted out of the circuit at one end. An ohmmeter supplies its own small current, so any other path in the circuit corrupts the reading.

Before you power anything up

Check the meter's function switch and which jacks the leads are in. Most fuse failures happen because the leads were still in the current jacks from the last measurement while the dial had been turned to volts.

Readings that will not match exactly

Your measured current will be a little off from your calculated current, and that is not a mistake on your part. Three reasons, all worth naming in your lab report:

  • The resistor's band. A 5% 1.0 kΩ resistor is legitimately anything from 950 Ω to 1 050 Ω.
  • The supply is not exactly at its dial setting. Measure it rather than assuming it.
  • The ammeter's own resistance adds a small amount to the circuit, which lowers the current slightly.

A good lab report states the calculated value, the measured value, the percent difference, and which of these accounts for it. A report claiming the numbers matched exactly when they did not is worth less than one that explains a 3% discrepancy correctly.

Percent difference

uses the calculated value as the reference:

% difference = |measured − calculated| ÷ calculated × 100

If your resistor is 5% tolerance and your meter is rated at 1%, a result within about 6% is consistent with the theory. Much beyond that and something is wired wrong, or the supply is not where you think it is.

Carry this into the report

The lab report outcome for this course asks you to interpret data, not just record it. The interpretation for this lab is one sentence: does the measured current agree with Ohm's law within the tolerance of the parts you used? Answer that explicitly and the rest of the report writes itself.

3.8Troubleshooting with Ohm's law

A circuit that misbehaves is telling you something. Ohm's law is how you translate it. If you know what the voltages and currents should be, then every reading that disagrees narrows down where the fault is.

Analysis, planning, measurement

Floyd calls this the , and it is worth following in that order rather than reaching for the meter first.

  1. Analysis. Think about what the circuit should do. Calculate the expected readings before you touch anything, so you have something to compare against.
  2. Planning. Decide where you will measure and in what order. A plan that halves the possibilities with each measurement beats one that walks through components left to right.
  3. Measurement. Take the readings, compare them to your expectations, and revise.

Half-splitting

: if a series string has eight components and one has failed, measuring at the midpoint tells you which half the fault is in. Repeat and you have found it in three measurements instead of eight. The technique costs nothing but a moment of planning.

The two failures you will see most

An is a break in the path. Current stops everywhere in a series circuit, so every good component drops zero volts, and the entire source voltage appears across the break. That last part surprises people: the failed component shows the highest reading on the meter, not the lowest.

A is an unintended low-resistance path. The shorted component drops zero volts, total resistance falls, current rises, and every other component drops more voltage than it should.

Key terms

Ohm's law
The relationship stating that current is directly proportional to voltage and inversely proportional to resistance.
Linear
Characterized by a straight-line relationship. In a linear resistor, a change in current is proportional to the change in voltage.
Directly proportional
Two quantities that rise and fall together by the same factor. Current and voltage, at fixed resistance.
Inversely proportional
Two quantities where one rises as the other falls by the same factor. Current and resistance, at fixed voltage.
Troubleshooting
The systematic process of identifying and locating a fault in a circuit or system.
Half-splitting
A troubleshooting method that eliminates half of the remaining possibilities with each measurement.
Open
A break in the current path. Current stops; the full source voltage appears across the break.
Short
An unintended low-resistance path. The shorted element drops no voltage and total current rises.

Ohm's Law — ELEC 103 Chapter 3

The three forms

To find currentI = V / R
To find voltageV = I × R
To find resistanceR = V / I

Written in base units: volts, amperes, ohms.

Prefix shortcuts that skip the conversion

volts ÷ kilohms= milliamperes
milliamperes × kilohms= volts
volts ÷ milliamperes= kilohms
volts ÷ ohms= amperes

12 V ÷ 4.7 kΩ = 2.55 mA, with nothing to convert.

Prefixes you will meet in this chapter

k (kilo)× 1 000
m (milli)÷ 1 000
µ (micro)÷ 1 000 000

1 kΩ = 1 000 Ω · 1 mA = 0.001 A · 1 000 µA = 1 mA

What the graphs look like

I against V, fixed R. A straight line through the origin. Slope = 1/R. Smaller resistor, steeper line.

I against R, fixed V. A curve that drops steeply then flattens. Never reaches zero.

Straight line = linear component. A lamp bends, because its filament heats up.

Reading a faulty series circuit

One resistor reads the full source voltage, all others read 0 Vthat one is open
One resistor reads 0 V, all others read higher than calculatedthat one is shorted
Readings match your calculations and sum to the sourceno fault

Analysis. Calculate what every reading should be, before touching the meter.

Planning. Choose measurements that cut the possibilities in half — not left to right.

Measurement. Read, compare to your expectation, revise.

How to connect the meter

Voltageacross the component, circuit left intact

Mistakes that cost the most points

  • Dividing the wrong pair. Name the quantity you want first.
  • Feeding kilohms into the formula without converting.
  • Answer off by exactly 1 000 — that is always a prefix slip.
  • Treating a lamp as a fixed resistance.
  • Reporting more significant figures than the given values support.
