5.1Recognizing a series connection

Components are when they are connected end to end so that there is exactly one path for current. Trace from the source terminal: if you can get all the way back without ever choosing between two routes, everything you passed through is in series.

That definition does more work than it looks. It says nothing about how the drawing is arranged. A series string can be drawn as a neat rectangle, an L-shape, or scattered across the page, and it is still series as long as there is one path.

Series or not?

Is every resistor in this circuit in series?

The test that always works

Put your finger on one terminal of the source and trace. Every time you reach a point where the wire splits, stop — those branches are not in series with each other. If you complete the loop without ever splitting, you have a series circuit.

Why it matters immediately

Everything in this chapter follows from the single-path property. One path means one current, and one current is what makes the total resistance add, the voltage drops divide, and Kirchhoff's voltage law hold. Misidentify the connection and every calculation after it is wrong.

5.2Total resistance adds

Resistors in series add. That is the whole rule:

RT = R1 + R2 + R3 + …

The reason is physical. Current has to pass through every resistor in turn, so each one adds its own opposition to the same single path. It is the same as making one long resistor out of several short ones.

Worked example 1

Three resistors of 680 Ω, 1.5 kΩ, and 2.2 kΩ are connected in series. Find the total resistance in ohms.

RT = R1 + R2 + R3 RT = 680 + 1 500 + 2 200 RT = 4 380 Ω = 4.38 kΩ

Convert everything to ohms before adding. This is the metric arithmetic rule from section 1.5, and it is where most errors in this chapter start.

Two useful consequences

The total is always larger than the largest single resistor — if it is not, you have made an arithmetic error. And equal resistors in series simply multiply: five 100 Ω resistors in series are 500 Ω.

5.3One path, one current

In a series circuit the current is the same at every point. Not similar — identical. An ammeter inserted anywhere in the loop reads the same value.

Students often expect current to be "used up" as it goes around, so that less arrives at the last resistor than at the first. It does not. Charge is not consumed; energy is. What each resistor takes is a share of the voltage, and that is section 5.6.

Find the current the same way you always do, with Ohm's law applied to the whole circuit:

I = VS / RT

5.4Voltage sources in series

Sources in series add algebraically — algebraically meaning you have to respect the polarity. Three 9 V batteries connected in the same direction give 27 V. Turn one of them around and it opposes the other two, so you get 9 V instead.

Three cells in a holder

27.0 V

The most common battery-holder fault

One cell inserted backwards. In a three-cell holder the pack reads one third of its expected voltage; in a four-cell holder it reads half. If a battery-powered circuit is behaving as though the supply is weak, check the cell orientation before you check anything else.

Why this comes up in the lab

You will sometimes need a voltage your single supply cannot produce, and stacking sources in series is how you get it. Two 12 V supplies in series give 24 V — but only if their grounds are not tied together, which is a real constraint on many bench supplies. Ask before you wire it.

5.5Kirchhoff's voltage law

states that the sum of the voltage drops around a closed path equals the source voltage in that path. Stated the other way, the algebraic sum of all voltages around any closed loop is zero.

VS = V1 + V2 + V3 + …

It is a statement about energy. Every coulomb of charge is given a fixed amount of energy by the source, and by the time it returns it has spent all of it. It cannot spend more than it was given, and it cannot keep any.

Worked example 2

A 12 V source drives three resistors in series. Two of the drops measure 4.5 V and 4.5 V. What is the third?

VS = V1 + V2 + V3 12 = 4.5 + 4.5 + V3 V3 = 3.0 V

Where you will actually use it

Two places, constantly. As a check: add up your calculated drops and see whether they come to the source voltage. And as a solver: when you know every drop but one, KVL gives you the last one without any further work.

KVL applies to one closed path at a time

In a series circuit the whole circuit is one closed path, so there is nothing to choose. From Chapter 7 onward there will be several loops, and you must apply KVL to one of them at a time. Building the habit now of naming the loop you are working on will pay off then.

5.6The voltage divider rule

Because the current is common, each resistor's voltage is its share of the total resistance, multiplied by the source voltage:

Vx = (Rx / RT) × VS

This is not a new law. It is Ohm's law applied twice with the current cancelled out. But it saves a step, and more importantly it makes the proportion visible: a resistor that is 40% of the total resistance takes 40% of the source voltage.

Only the ratio sets the output

Two 10 kΩ resistors and two 100 Ω resistors both give exactly half the source voltage. What changes is the current drawn: 100 Ω resistors waste a hundred times more power doing the same job. Pick the ratio for the voltage you want and the magnitude for the current you can afford.

5.7Dividers in practice

A fixed divider gives one output voltage. Replace the two resistors with a potentiometer and you get a variable one — this is what a volume control does, and it is the circuit behind most of the adjustment knobs you will ever meet.

Where dividers appear

  • Setting a reference. A circuit needs 2.5 V from a 5 V supply; two equal resistors provide it.
  • Sensors. A thermistor or photoresistor in series with a fixed resistor turns a changing resistance into a changing voltage that a circuit can read.
  • Scaling a signal down. A 10× oscilloscope probe is a voltage divider, which is exactly why the reading has to be multiplied back up.
  • Adjustment. Volume, brightness, and trim controls.

The catch that Chapter 7 will explain

Connect a load across the output of a divider and the output voltage drops. The load is in parallel with the lower resistor, which changes the ratio. A divider built from large resistances is more affected than one built from small ones. For now, note that an unloaded divider and a loaded one behave differently — section 7 covers the arithmetic.

5.8Power in a series circuit

Each resistor dissipates its own power, and the total is their sum:

PT = P1 + P2 + P3 + …

You can also get the total directly from the source: PT = VS × I. Both routes must give the same answer, which makes this a useful check on your other work.

Because the current is common to every resistor, P = I²R is usually the easiest form to use here. And since I is the same for all of them, the resistor with the largest resistance dissipates the most power. In a series string, the biggest resistor gets hottest.

Worked example 3

A 12 V source drives 680 Ω, 1.5 kΩ, and 2.2 kΩ in series. Find the total power in milliwatts.

RT = 4 380 Ω, so I = 12 ÷ 4 380 = 2.74 mA PT = VS × I = 12 × 0.00274 PT = 0.0329 W = 32.9 mW

Check it the long way: 5.1 mW + 11.3 mW + 16.5 mW = 32.9 mW. The two agree, which is the point.

Choosing ratings in a series string

Size each resistor for its own dissipation, not for the total. Applying Chapter 4's factor of two to the largest resistor in the string is usually enough, because it is the one doing the most work — but calculate each one when the values are close together.

5.9Ground and how voltages are named

Section 2.2 said voltage is always between two points. In practice, naming both points every time is tedious, so circuits designate one point as the reference and measure everything against it. That reference is called .

Ground is a choice, not a physical property. Moving the reference to a different point changes every voltage you would quote, without changing the circuit at all. That is the idea this section exists to make concrete.

Move the ground

Subscript notation

  • One subscript means with respect to ground. VA is the voltage at point A relative to the reference.
  • Two subscripts mean between those two points. VAB is the voltage at A relative to B, and it equals VA − VB.

Notice in the tool that the two-subscript voltages never change when you move the ground. Only the single-subscript ones do. The difference between two points is a property of the circuit; the value at a single point is a property of where you put your reference.

Three meanings of the word

Earth ground is a physical connection to the earth, for safety. Chassis ground is the metal enclosure used as a common conductor. Reference ground is the circuit point everything is measured against. They are often connected, and they are often not. When a schematic says ground, it means the third one unless it says otherwise.

5.10Faults in a series circuit

Chapter 3 introduced open and short failures. A series circuit makes both of them easy to find, because the single path means a fault anywhere affects everything.

An open

Current stops everywhere. Every good resistor drops zero volts, because zero current through any resistance is zero volts. The entire source voltage appears across the break. The failed part shows the highest reading on the meter, not the lowest — which still surprises people the first several times.

A short

The shorted component drops zero volts. Total resistance falls, so the current rises, so every remaining resistor drops more than it should. Nothing reads zero except the fault itself.

The two signatures are opposites, and together they let you find any single fault in a string with a few measurements.

Half-splitting still applies

With four resistors, measuring across two of them at once tells you which half the fault is in. Two more measurements find it exactly. Guessing component by component takes twice as long on average and much longer on a string of eight.

Working a series circuit

  • Add the resistances to get Rₜ.
  • I = Vₛ ÷ Rₜ — this current is common.
  • Each drop is V = I × R for that resistor.
  • Check: the drops must sum to Vₛ.

Vₓ = (Rₓ / Rₜ) × Vₛ

A resistor takes the same share of the voltage as it holds of the total resistance. Only the ratio sets the output; the magnitude sets the current drawn.
