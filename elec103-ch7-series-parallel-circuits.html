7.1Seeing the structure

Nothing new is introduced in this chapter. There is no fourth law and no new formula. Every circuit here is solved with the rules from Chapters 5 and 6 — the work is deciding which rule applies to which part, and in what order.

That decision is a skill, and it is almost entirely about reading the topology rather than doing arithmetic. Get the structure right and the numbers follow mechanically. Get it wrong and no amount of careful calculation will save the answer.

The question to ask, repeatedly

Look for the smallest group you can reduce, not the whole circuit at once. Two questions, applied over and over:

  • Do these two share both nodes? Then they are in parallel and you can replace them with one equivalent resistor.
  • Do these two form an unbroken path with nothing branching off between them? Then they are in series and you can add them.

Replace the group with its equivalent, redraw, and ask again. Every combination circuit in this course collapses to a single resistor this way.

The mistake that ruins the whole problem

Calling two resistors parallel because they are drawn side by side, or series because they are drawn end to end. Neither drawing tells you anything. Two resistors are in parallel only if both ends land on the same two , and in series only if no third component connects between them. Trace the wire every time until it becomes automatic.

Guided problem 1 · reading the structure

R1 is 1.0 kΩ. It is in series with the parallel combination of R2 = 2.2 kΩ and R3 = 3.3 kΩ. What is the total resistance in ohms?

  • Do not add all three. Decide which pair belongs together first, and reduce that pair before touching R1.
  • R2 and R3 share both nodes, so they are the parallel pair. R1 is in series with whatever they combine to.
  1. Combine the parallel pair first, using product over sum: (2 200 × 3 300) ÷ 5 500 = 1 320 Ω.
  2. Check it: 1 320 Ω is below 2 200 Ω, the smaller of the two, as a parallel result must be.
  3. Now R1 is in series with that 1 320 Ω, so add: 1 000 + 1 320.
  4. The total is 2 320 Ω. Adding all three straight off would have given 6 500 Ω — nearly three times too large, and the most common error in this chapter.

7.2Reducing to an equivalent

An is one that behaves identically at its terminals but is simpler inside. No measurement made from outside can tell the difference — that is what equivalent means, and it is why the substitution is legitimate.

works from the inside out: start furthest from the source, collapse one group at a time, and stop when a single resistance remains. Then work back outward to find the individual voltages and currents.

Redraw at every step

The single most useful habit in this chapter is physically redrawing the simplified circuit after each reduction, rather than tracking it in your head. It takes ten seconds and it makes the next grouping obvious. Every worked solution you will ever read does this, and every student who skips it loses track by the second step.

Guided problem 2 · one reduction step

R3 = 1.5 kΩ and R4 = 1.0 kΩ sit end to end in one branch. That branch is in parallel with R2 = 2.2 kΩ. What single resistance is equivalent to those three, in ohms?

  • Work from the inside out. R3 and R4 are the innermost group — combine them before anything else.
  • Once R3 and R4 become one resistance, the problem is just two resistors in parallel.
  1. R3 and R4 are end to end with nothing branching between them, so they are in series: 1 500 + 1 000 = 2 500 Ω.
  2. Call that Ra. Now Ra and R2 share both nodes, so they are in parallel.
  3. Product over sum: (2 500 × 2 200) ÷ (2 500 + 2 200) = 5 500 000 ÷ 4 700.
  4. The equivalent is 1 170 Ω. Redraw at this point and the circuit has one resistor where three used to be.

7.3Solving the whole circuit

Once the circuit is reduced you have the total resistance, and from it the total current. Then you expand back outward, using the series and parallel rules in reverse to find each individual quantity.

The order that always works

  1. Reduce to a single equivalent resistance, RT.
  2. Total current from the source: IT = VS / RT.
  3. Work outward. Series elements carry the current you already know; parallel groups share the voltage you already know.
  4. Check with around a loop and KCL at a node.

Guided problem 3 · solving the whole circuit

A 24 V source drives R1 = 1.0 kΩ in series with a parallel section made of R2 = 2.2 kΩ and a branch of R3 = 1.5 kΩ plus R4 = 1.0 kΩ. What voltage appears across the parallel section?

  • Reduce all the way to a single resistance first, then find the total current, then work back outward.
  • Once you know the total current, the drop across R1 follows from Ohm’s law, and KVL gives you the rest.
  1. From the previous problem, the parallel section reduces to 1 170 Ω. Total: Rₜ = 1 000 + 1 170 = 2 170 Ω.
  2. Total current: Iₜ = 24 V ÷ 2 170 Ω = 11.06 mA.
  3. That current passes through R1, dropping V₁ = 11.06 mA × 1.0 kΩ = 11.06 V.
  4. By KVL the rest appears across the parallel section: 24 − 11.06 = 12.9 V. Check it directly: 11.06 mA × 1 170 Ω = 12.9 V.

7.4Checking with Kirchhoff's laws

In a series or parallel circuit the checks were almost automatic. In a combination circuit they are the difference between an answer you trust and one you hope is right.

Both laws apply to any circuit, not just the simple ones. What changes here is that you have to pick which loop or which node you are applying them to.

KVL, around a chosen loop

Pick any closed path — including one that goes through a parallel branch — and the drops around it must equal the source voltage in that path. In the circuit from section 7.3 there are two loops that include the source, and either works as a check.

KCL, at a chosen node

At the node where the parallel section begins, the current arriving from the series element must equal the sum of the branch currents leaving it. If those do not match to within your rounding, something upstream is wrong.

Worked example 1

A total of 6 mA arrives at a node that splits into two branches. One branch is measured at 4 mA. What does the other carry?

Iin = I1 + I2 6 = 4 + I2 I2 = 2 mA

Why checking matters more here

A wrong reduction usually still produces plausible-looking numbers. The circuit does not complain, the units work out, and the answer is simply wrong. KVL and are the only inexpensive way to catch that before it reaches your lab report or an exam page.

7.5Power in a combination circuit

The rule has not changed: total power is the sum of the individual powers, and it also equals the source voltage multiplied by the total current.

PT = VS × IT = P1 + P2 + P3 + …

What has changed is that you can no longer say which resistor runs hottest by inspection. In a series circuit it was the largest; in a parallel circuit the smallest; in a combination circuit it depends on both the resistance and how much of the current or voltage that part actually gets. Calculate each one.

Where the heat concentrates

Often it is the series element closest to the source, because every bit of current in the circuit passes through it while the parallel branches each take only a share. When you are choosing power ratings for a combination circuit, check that component first — but check the others too.

Worked example 2

A 24 V source drives a combination circuit that draws 12 mA in total. Find the total power in milliwatts.

PT = VS × IT PT = 24 V × 0.012 A PT = 0.288 W = 288 mW

You do not need the internal structure at all for the total. You need it only to find how that power is distributed.

Guided problem 4 · power in the series element

In that same circuit — 24 V, R1 = 1.0 kΩ, total resistance 2 170 Ω — how much power does R1 dissipate, in milliwatts?

  • Every bit of the circuit’s current passes through R1, which makes P = I²R the natural form.
  • Find the total current first, then square it. Square only the current, not the whole product.
  1. Total current: I = 24 ÷ 2 170 = 0.01106 A.
  2. Square it: 0.01106² = 0.0001223.
  3. Multiply by R1: 0.0001223 × 1 000 = 0.1223 W.
  4. R1 dissipates 122 mW. It is the hottest component here because all the current goes through it, while the parallel branches each take only a share.

7.6The loading effect of a voltmeter

Chapter 5 promised this and deferred it. Here it is: connecting a voltmeter across part of a circuit changes that circuit, because the meter's own resistance goes in parallel with whatever you are measuring.

The meter reads the voltage that exists while it is connected, which is not quite the voltage that was there before. This is called , and it is a real measurement error, not a fault.

When it matters and when it does not

The error depends on the ratio between the meter's resistance and the circuit's. A 10 MΩ meter across a 1 kΩ divider is invisible — the error is far below the meter's own accuracy. The same meter across a 1 MΩ divider is a serious problem.

As a working rule, loading is negligible when the meter's resistance is at least a hundred times the resistance it is placed across. Below about ten times, the reading is not trustworthy without correcting for it.

Analog meters load much harder

An analog meter's input resistance is given in and depends on the range you select. At 20 kΩ/V on a 10 V range it presents 200 kΩ — a thousand times worse than a typical digital meter, and enough to noticeably disturb a high-resistance circuit. Your lab has both kinds. Know which one you are holding.

The same idea, everywhere

An ammeter has the mirror problem: its small but non-zero resistance adds to the circuit and slightly reduces the current it is measuring. And in Chapter 5, a load connected across a divider output pulled that output down for exactly this reason. All three are one phenomenon — connecting something changes the thing you connected to.

Guided problem 5 · what the meter actually reads

The divider below is built from two 100 kΩ resistors across 10 V, so the true voltage across R2 is 5.00 V. A voltmeter with 1 MΩ internal resistance is connected across R2. What will it read?

  • The meter’s resistance goes in parallel with R2 while it is connected. Work out that combination first.
  • Then treat the circuit as a fresh divider between R1 and the new, lower lower-half resistance.
  1. R2 in parallel with the meter: (100 k × 1 M) ÷ 1.1 M = 90.9 kΩ.
  2. The divider is now 100 kΩ on top and 90.9 kΩ underneath, total 190.9 kΩ.
  3. Apply the divider rule: 10 V × (90.9 ÷ 190.9).
  4. The meter reads 4.76 V, not 5.00 V — an error of 4.8%. The meter is only ten times the resistance it is across, which is well inside the range where loading matters.

7.7The Wheatstone bridge

A is two voltage dividers connected across the same source, with the measurement taken between their outputs. It is the most useful circuit ever devised, and it works on a principle worth understanding.

When the two dividers produce the same fraction, the voltage between their outputs is zero. The bridge is then , and at balance:

RX = R3 × (R2 / R1)

Why null measurement is so accurate

At balance you are not measuring a voltage at all — you are detecting the absence of one. That removes the accuracy of the detector from the result entirely. A cheap meter that can reliably tell zero from not-zero gives an answer as good as your known resistors are, and precision resistors are far easier to obtain than precision meters.

The unbalanced bridge

The other half of its usefulness. Put a sensor in one arm — a strain gauge, a thermistor, a load cell — and the bridge output becomes a voltage proportional to how far that sensor has moved from its resting value. Every electronic scale you have ever stood on works this way.

Note what it is made of

Two dividers in parallel across one source. Nothing in this circuit is beyond Chapters 5 and 6 — which is the point of the whole chapter. Combination circuits are not a new subject, they are the earlier subjects applied together.

Guided problem 6 · measuring with a bridge

The bridge below is balanced, with R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 1.0 kΩ and R3 = 6.8 kΩ. What is the unknown resistance Rₓ, in ohms?

  • At balance the two dividers produce the same fraction, which gives Rₓ = R₃ × (R₂ / R₁).
  • R2 divided by R1 is a ratio less than one here, so expect Rₓ to come out smaller than R₃.
  1. At balance, Rₓ = R₃ × (R₂ / R₁).
  2. The ratio arms give R₂ / R₁ = 1 000 ÷ 10 000 = 0.1.
  3. So Rₓ = 6 800 × 0.1.
  4. The unknown is 680 Ω. Note what the answer depends on: three known resistors and nothing else. The detector only had to tell zero from not-zero, so its own accuracy never entered the result.

Self-check

Where this course ends, and what follows

You can now analyze any resistive DC circuit that reduces by inspection, take the measurements that verify it, and recognize when the act of measuring has disturbed the result. That is the whole of ELEC 103.

The next course begins where inspection stops working. Some circuits cannot be reduced by combining series and parallel groups at all, and Chapters 8 and 9 introduce the methods for those — superposition, Thevenin's theorem, and branch, loop, and node analysis. After that come magnetism, capacitors, inductors, and transformers, and the third course takes all of it into alternating current.

Everything ahead rests on Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's two laws, and the ability to look at a schematic and see the structure. You have all three.

How to attack any combination circuit

  • Find the smallest group you can combine.
  • Reduce it and redraw.
  • Repeat until one resistance remains.
  • Iₜ = Vₛ ÷ Rₜ.
  • Work back outward for individual values.
  • Check with KVL and KCL.
