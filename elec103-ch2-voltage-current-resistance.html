2.1Where charge comes from

Electric current is moving charge. To understand why charge moves easily through copper and not at all through rubber, you have to look at the atom — specifically at its outermost electrons.

The Bohr model

Niels Bohr pictured the atom as a nucleus of positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons, with negatively charged electrons in discrete shells around it. It is not the modern picture, but it is exactly the right level of detail for electronics, and it predicts the one thing this course cares about: how tightly the outer electrons are held.

The is the outermost occupied shell. The electrons in it are furthest from the nucleus, held most weakly, and are the ones that can be persuaded to leave.

Valence and conduction

The rule of four

Count the valence electrons and you can predict the electrical behavior.

  • Fewer than four: the atom gives electrons up easily. It is a conductor. Copper has one.
  • More than four: the atom holds its electrons tightly. It is an insulator.
  • Exactly four: neither. These are the semiconductors, and silicon is the one the whole industry runs on.

An electron that escapes its atom entirely becomes a . In a copper wire there are enormous numbers of them drifting at random. Apply a voltage and that random drift acquires a direction, and you have current.

Charge and Coulomb's law

Charge is measured in , symbol Q. One coulomb is the charge of about 6.25 × 1018 electrons — the sort of number section 1.3 taught you to write properly.

Coulomb's law says the force between two charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. That force is what a voltage source works against when it pushes charge around a circuit.

Why copper and not gold

Silver conducts better than copper and gold resists corrosion better than either, so neither is chosen on physics alone. Copper wins on cost per unit of conductivity. Gold still appears where a connection must not corrode — the contacts on a circuit board edge connector, for instance — because a thin plating is cheap and a bad contact is not.

Guided problem 1 · counting charge

One coulomb is the charge carried by about 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons. How many electrons make up 4.0 C of charge? Enter the coefficient, the number that multiplies 10¹⁸.

  • This is a straight proportion. Four coulombs is four times the charge of one coulomb.
  • Multiply the coefficient and leave the power of ten alone — nothing here changes the exponent.
  1. One coulomb is 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons.
  2. Four coulombs is 4.0 × that: 4.0 × 6.25 = 25.
  3. The exponent does not change, so the answer is 25 × 10¹⁸.
  4. That is 25 × 10¹⁸ electrons, or 2.5 × 10¹⁹ in strict scientific notation.

2.2Voltage

is energy per unit of charge. Formally:

V = W / Q

where W is energy in joules and Q is charge in coulombs. One volt is one joule per coulomb. A 12 V battery gives every coulomb that passes through it 12 joules to spend on the way round the circuit.

Voltage is always between two points

This is the idea people most often skip, and it causes trouble for the rest of the course. There is no such thing as the voltage at a point, only the voltage between two points, in the same way that a single stair has no height until you say what you are measuring it against.

That is why a voltmeter has two leads and why you place them on either side of a component. If someone says "the voltage at the collector," they mean with respect to ground, and ground is the second point they left unsaid.

Worked example 1

Moving 4.5 C of charge through a component takes 40.5 J of energy. What is the voltage across it?

V = W / Q V = 40.5 J ÷ 4.5 C V = 9.0 V

Two words for the same thing

You will see voltage called potential difference and , or emf. All three name the same quantity. Older texts and European sources prefer the letter E where this book uses V. If you meet E = IR in your reading, it is Ohm's law with different clothes on.

Guided problem 2 · voltage from energy and charge

Moving 2.5 C of charge through a component requires 30 J of energy. What is the voltage across it?

  • Voltage is energy per unit of charge. Which of the two given values is the energy?
  • V = W / Q. Put the joules on top and the coulombs underneath.
  1. Write the definition: V = W / Q.
  2. Substitute: V = 30 J ÷ 2.5 C.
  3. Divide: 30 ÷ 2.5 = 12.
  4. The voltage is 12 V. Every coulomb passing through gives up 12 joules.

2.3Sources of voltage

Every source converts some other form of energy into electrical energy. Knowing which form tells you what the source is good at and how it fails.

Where the energy comes from

Select a source to see how it works and where it is used.

The one you will actually use

Almost every measurement in this course comes from the bench power supply, which converts AC from the wall into a regulated DC output you can dial in. Regulated means it holds its set voltage as the changes — within limits. Exceed its current limit and it will either fold back the voltage or shut down, depending on the model.

Get in the habit of measuring the supply output rather than trusting the dial. The two disagree more often than you would expect, and section 1.6 explains why that is not a defect.

Batteries in series, and one wired backwards

Voltage sources in series add. Three 9 V batteries in series give 27 V. Reverse one of them and it subtracts instead: you get 9 V, not 27. In a battery holder, one cell inserted backwards is the most common reason a circuit reads roughly a third of what it should.

Guided problem 3 · sources in series

Four 1.5 V cells are placed in a holder, all facing the same way, and then one of them is reversed. What is the total voltage of the pack?

  • Sources in series add, but a reversed source subtracts rather than adding.
  • Three cells are pushing one way and one is pushing against them.
  1. With all four facing the same way the pack would give 4 × 1.5 = 6.0 V.
  2. One cell reversed means three add and one subtracts: 1.5 + 1.5 + 1.5 − 1.5.
  3. That is 4.5 − 1.5.
  4. The pack gives 3.0 V, half of what it should. A reversed cell costs you twice its own voltage, because it stops adding and starts subtracting.

2.4Current

is the rate at which charge flows past a point:

I = Q / t

One ampere is one coulomb per second. Current is the only electrical quantity that is an SI base unit, and it is defined using time, another base unit.

Worked example 2

10 coulombs of charge pass a point in 5 seconds. What is the current?

I = Q / t I = 10 C ÷ 5 s I = 2.0 A

Current is measured through, not across

Because current is a flow past a point, an ammeter has to be in the path so that the flow goes through it. You break the circuit open and insert the meter into the gap. This is the practical difference that costs beginners meter fuses, and section 2.9 covers it in detail.

How fast do electrons actually move?

Slowly. In a typical wire, individual electrons drift at fractions of a millimeter per second. What travels near the speed of light is the electric field, not the electrons. The usual analogy is a pipe already full of water: open the tap and water comes out the far end immediately, but the particular molecule you added takes a long time to make the trip.

Typical magnitudes

An LED indicator runs on 10 to 20 mA. A phone charger delivers 1 to 3 A. A car starter motor draws hundreds of amperes for a second or two. Anything above roughly 10 mA through a person is dangerous, which is a much smaller number than most of the ones above.

Guided problem 4 · current, charge and time

A component carries a steady current of 250 mA. How long does it take for 6.0 C of charge to pass through it?

  • Start from I = Q / t and rearrange it for the quantity you actually want.
  • The current is given in milliamperes but the formula works in base units. Convert first.
  1. I = Q / t, so rearranged for time, t = Q / I.
  2. Convert the current to base units: 250 mA = 0.250 A.
  3. Substitute: t = 6.0 C ÷ 0.250 A.
  4. The time is 24 s. Leaving the current in milliamperes would have given 0.024, wrong by a factor of 1 000.

2.5Resistance and conductance

is opposition to current, measured in ohms. One ohm is the resistance that allows one ampere when one volt is applied — which is Ohm's law hiding inside a definition, and Chapter 3 will make it explicit.

is the reciprocal of resistance, measured in siemens. It answers the same question from the other side: not how much a circuit opposes current, but how readily it allows it. You will rarely use it in this course, but it appears in parallel-circuit formulas in Chapter 6.

Where resistance comes from

Four physical properties set the resistance of a conductor: the material, the length, the cross-sectional area, and the temperature. Longer means more resistance; thicker means less. That is why wire is specified by gauge — , American Wire Gauge — and why the numbering runs backwards, with a smaller gauge number meaning a thicker wire.

Wire is not a perfect conductor

Every wire, solder joint, switch contact, and meter lead has some resistance. In this course it is small enough to ignore, and the book will tell you to ignore it. Remember that you are choosing to, because in a high-current circuit that same resistance is what turns an undersized wire into a heater.

Resistors

A resistor is a component built to have a specific resistance on purpose. The ones on your bench are carbon film or metal film, rated in ohms and in watts, with a that says how far from the marked value the real one is allowed to be. The next section is about reading those markings.

Guided problem 5 · conductance

A component has a resistance of 250 Ω. What is its conductance in millisiemens?

  • Conductance is simply the reciprocal of resistance.
  • The answer is asked for in millisiemens, so you will need to convert after taking the reciprocal.
  1. G = 1 / R, so G = 1 ÷ 250.
  2. That gives 0.004 S.
  3. Milli means 10⁻³, so 0.004 S = 4 × 10⁻³ S.
  4. The conductance is 4 mS. A large resistance always means a small conductance, and the reverse.

2.6Reading a resistor

Resistors are too small to print a number on, so the value is coded in colored bands. Learning to read them is a genuine skill and one you will use in every lab this semester.

On a four-band resistor the first two bands are digits, the third is a multiplier, and the fourth is the tolerance. On a five-band resistor the first three are digits, then multiplier, then tolerance. Five-band parts are the precision ones.

If you cannot distinguish the colors

Roughly one man in twelve has some degree of color vision deficiency, and brown, red, and orange are exactly the confusion most often affects. This is not a barrier to working in electronics. Measure the resistor with an ohmmeter and label the strip or the bin — professionals do this routinely, because a faded band on an old part fools everyone. Tell your instructor if the bands are hard to read; the lab can be set up so that this never slows you down.

Which end do you start from?

The tolerance band is usually gold or silver and sits slightly further from the end. Put that band on your right and read left to right. If both ends look the same, measure it — guessing the direction turns a 220 Ω resistor into a 22 Ω one.

Alphanumeric labeling

Larger resistors and most schematics skip the colors and use a letter in place of the decimal point. The letter tells you the multiplier and its position tells you where the point goes.

  • 4R7 is 4.7 Ω
  • 4K7 is 4.7 kΩ
  • 47K is 47 kΩ
  • 1M0 is 1.0 MΩ

This scheme exists because a printed decimal point disappears on a photocopy or a worn label, and a misplaced one is a factor of ten. You will see it constantly on schematics from Chapter 5 onward.

Guided problem 6 · reading the color code

Read the resistor below. What is its nominal resistance in ohms?

  • The first two bands are digits and the third is a multiplier. The gold band on the right is the tolerance and takes no part in the value.
  • Black is 0 as a digit. Be careful: as a multiplier black would mean ×1, but here it is in a digit position.
  1. Brown in the first digit position is 1. Black in the second digit position is 0. Together they read 10.
  2. Orange in the multiplier position means × 1 000.
  3. 10 × 1 000 = 10 000.
  4. The resistor is 10 000 Ω, which you would say aloud as 10 k. The gold band tells you it may measure anywhere from 9 500 to 10 500 Ω.

2.7Variable resistors

A has three terminals: the two ends of a resistive track and a wiper that slides along it. The resistance from the wiper to either end depends on where the wiper sits, and the two always add up to the full track resistance.

A has two terminals and varies the resistance in a circuit. Any potentiometer can be wired as a rheostat by using the wiper and one end, and leaving the third terminal unconnected — or better, tying it to the wiper so a dirty track cannot open the circuit.

Where you will meet them

A volume control is a potentiometer. A dimmer is a rheostat. On your bench, the trimmer on a circuit board is a potentiometer set once at the factory and not meant to be adjusted casually.

Note that the resistance from A to B never changes no matter where the wiper sits. Only the split changes. Chapter 5 turns that fact into the voltage divider, which is one of the most useful circuits in the book.

Guided problem 7 · a potentiometer split

The potentiometer below has a 10 kΩ track and its wiper sits 30% of the way down from terminal A. What is the resistance between the wiper and terminal B?

  • The wiper divides the track into two parts that always add up to the full track resistance.
  • The wiper is 30% of the way from A, so the A-to-wiper part is 30% of the track. The question asks about the other part.
  1. The full track is 10 kΩ, and the wiper splits it in two.
  2. From A to the wiper is 30% of 10 kΩ = 3 kΩ.
  3. The two parts must sum to 10 kΩ, so wiper to B is 10 − 3 = 7 kΩ.
  4. The resistance from the wiper to B is 7 000 Ω. Note that A to B is still 10 kΩ no matter where the wiper sits — only the split changes.

2.8The basic circuit

Every working circuit has three things, and almost every one has a fourth.

  1. A source of voltage
  2. A load that does something with the energy
  3. A complete current path between them
  4. Usually, a means of control — a switch

A flashlight is the canonical example: battery, lamp, metal body forming the path, and a slide switch. Take away any one of the first three and nothing happens.

Open and closed

switch closed

Poles and throws

Switches are named by how many independent circuits they control and how many contacts each one can reach. A pole is a movable arm; a throw is a position it can be thrown to.

  • SPST — single pole, single throw. On or off. The flashlight switch.
  • SPDT — single pole, double throw. Selects between two destinations.
  • DPST and DPDT — the same, but switching two circuits at once with one action.

Reading a schematic

A shows how components connect, not where they physically sit. A wire on a schematic is a perfect conductor of zero length and zero resistance. Two lines crossing without a dot are not connected; a dot means a junction. Your breadboard layout will look nothing like the schematic and must be electrically identical to it, and reconciling those two pictures is most of what the first few labs teach.

Protective devices belong in the path too

A is a deliberate weak point placed in series so that it fails before anything expensive does. It is rated in amperes, and replacing a blown fuse with a higher-rated one removes the protection entirely. Your DMM has one in its current path, and you will meet it the first time someone connects the ammeter across a supply.

2.9The meter, and staying safe

The measures voltage, current, and resistance with the same two probes, and the three measurements are connected in completely different ways. Getting this wrong is the most common lab accident in the course.

The pattern behind the table: a voltmeter must not divert current, so it has high resistance. An ammeter must not obstruct current, so it has low resistance. Put the low-resistance one across a supply and you have deliberately built a short circuit.

Safety

The voltages on your bench are low, and the habits you build here are the ones you will carry to equipment where they are not.

  • Know where the emergency power shutoff is before you need it.
  • Current, not voltage, is what injures. Around 10 mA across the body is enough to prevent you from letting go.
  • Remove rings and watches. Metal jewelry across a low-voltage, high-current source causes serious burns.
  • Electrical fires need a Class C extinguisher. Water conducts.
  • Keep the bench clear. Most lab injuries start as clutter.

The habit that saves the fuse

Leave the meter in its voltage function and the leads in the voltage jacks whenever you are not actively measuring current. Nearly every blown meter fuse happens because the leads were still in the current jacks from the last measurement while the dial had been turned back to volts.
